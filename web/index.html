      Analytic model vs full engine

      (μ+λ) evolution on the strategy weights. Fitness is smoothed RP plus win rate plus point margin, and selection is paired: every candidate, the incumbent included, is scored on the same MATCHes each generation, so "improved" never means "measured on easier seeds". The result line prints the selection noise it measured — the spread between candidates on identical MATCHes — and a gain smaller than about twice it is the seed set, not the weights. The browser budget is small, so treat a run here as exploratory; scripts/train.mjs is the same search offline and refuses to return weights that do not beat the shipped set on seed families it never trained on.

The right column says which function implements it. A self-check enforces Table 6-3 and the score identity at load.

      Calibrated against real matchesgreedy mirror 615.7 against a real average of 627 — 1.8% under

      The table above is the real scoreboard of the 2026 Einstein Finals (2026cmptx, read from The Blue Alliance): 456 / 518, 711 / 561, 712 / 406 — average 627, high 842. Simulated scores have to land near those or the tactics mean nothing.

      The comparable run is the greedy mirror: the same hand-written cycling plan on both sides, nobody defending, 24 MATCHes × 2 seed sets, and now the same three ROBOTs on both ALLIANCES. It scores 615.7 (611.3 / 620.1 for the two sample trios), which is 1.8% under the real average. The AI's own searched mirror is 606.2 (592.5 / 619.9) — 9.5 points under greedy's, because in a mirror the two sides' denial cancels but the search still pays for thinking about it (the same denial is worth +8.3 win points against a baseline that cannot cancel it; see "How strong the AI is"). A mirror pinned to all-SCORE is 662.0 (629.7 / 694.3). Both sides of a mirror share one random stream, so read all three as ±2%.

      Earlier builds got this wrong twice over. A "mirror" used to mean one trio on each side, so every mirror was also a roster comparison, and the red and blue numbers on this page were comparing 254/1678/1690 against 4414/5940/11019 — which on the identical policy is worth 59.8 points. It also meant the engine was serving the two ALLIANCES in a fixed order and opening the midfield in a lattice that was not symmetric. Both are fixed, and the outlet cap was re-derived after them on the symmetric field: there it is 4.2 FUEL/s, where the old 4.0 now prints 598.6 and 4.2 prints this 615.7.

      Throughput still runs high: 5.44 FUEL/s alliance-wide against a real ≈4.7. The field carries a mean of 226 loose FUEL (minimum 146) of the 504 §6.3.4 stages: 24 per DEPOT on the carpet, 24 per CHUTE held behind the CHUTE DOOR, up to 8 preloaded per ROBOT, 360 in the NEUTRAL ZONE. The HUB ejects what it processes back into the NEUTRAL ZONE through four outlets (§5.4), which is why the loose FUEL ends up midfield. Every ball is drawn in the replay.

      Why red and blue are not 60 points apartone roster confound and two engine bugs, plus what is left

      This page used to say that with the identical plan on both sides, red scored 592.5 and blue 652.3. That was not a seat effect. A "mirror" then meant one trio on each side, so the sentence was comparing 254/1678/1690 against 4414/5940/11019 — and the same policy on those two rosters is worth 59.8 points. Every mirror now flies one trio on BOTH alliances, and ab.mjs and league.js pair the same way, which is also what turned the AI's headline win rate from 26.3% into 79.7%.

      Under the roster there were two real asymmetries, and both were in the engine.

      Who gets served first. The per-tick loop walked the ROBOT array, and the red ROBOTS are built first, so red resolved its INTAKE claims before blue ever saw the same FUEL. Two identical greedy policies scored 598.6 against 630.5. The tick loop now alternates the alliance it serves each tick, which alone did nothing: decisions happen every 0.2 s, so the order has to alternate on the decision clock, not the tick. With both alternations the same comparison is 611.0 against 615.6.

      Where the midfield FUEL sits. The 360-ball NEUTRAL ZONE layout was built row by row from one corner of the box, so the piles were nearer one HUB than the other, and every policy that chases nearest-pile was handed an unfair schedule. The layout is now point-symmetric about the field centre: an even number of rows, columns centred, each ball placed with its mirror partner and sharing one jitter draw so the pair lands or fails together, and the odd ball (if the count is odd) goes on the centre point first so no pair can be pushed off it. That is what made the rear-intake advantage disappear: a back mouth on a tank had measured +20 points, and it was the lattice, not the face.

      Re-deriving the HUB outlet cap after those two fixes moved the shipped value from 4.0 to 4.2 FUEL/s — the symmetric field drains a little differently, and the anchor is what the cap is backed out from.

      What is left. The greedy mirror is symmetric now (−1.3 ± 7.2 over 96 MATCHes, and the pass reach does not touch it — greedy never throws). The pinned all-SCORE mirror is not: it splits +30.4 ± 6.0, about 5.0 standard errors. It read +15.2 ± 5.3 on the build before the pass reach, which the control run reproduces on the same seeds, so the direct pass did not create the seat gap — it roughly doubled it. The difference between the two mirrors is which micro policy the ROBOTS fly, so whatever is left is in the shipped policy's interaction with the seat, not in the field, and this is the second measurement saying the policy's far-half behaviour is where the asymmetry lives. It is not localised yet; scripts/seat.mjs prints the per-side decomposition (collected / scored / wasted / blocked / impaired, per ALLIANCE and per AUTO outcome) that found the two engine bugs, and that is where it goes next.

      Reproduce with node scripts/mirror.mjs (prints both seat tests) and TR=0 MATCHES=24 node scripts/seat.mjs.

      How strong the AI is76.0% of 96 MATCHes against greedy, 662.7 to 623.7

      Against greedy — a hand-written plan that never wastes FUEL, cycles the nearest pile and climbs at the end — the AI wins 76.0% of 96 MATCHes over four disjoint seed families (83.3 / 70.8 / 79.2 / 70.8%) and scores 662.7 against 623.7 on identical ROBOTs. Both sides now fly the same three cards, so that margin is a plan, not a roster. It used to read 79.7% (87.5 / 81.3 / 79.2 / 70.8%, 666.3 to 622.7) and the build before the pass reach landed still prints exactly that on these seeds; what moved it is Careful being allowed to throw a full hopper home instead of carrying it. 3.7 win points is under one standard error of a 96-match difference, so the honest statement is that the direct pass costs somewhere between nothing and about 7 points, not that it costs 3.7.

      The whole of it is still the macro search. Pin every ROBOT to "always cycle" and the win rate falls to 60.9% and the margin to 12.3 — while the AI's own score goes up, to 676.1. So the search spends about 13.4 points of its own FUEL to buy +15.1 points of win rate: it is buying denial, and against a baseline that cannot punish it, that is a trade that wins MATCHes. Taking DEFEND out of the micro policy no longer separates at all: the same command prints 79.7% for that arm against the base 76.0%, so removing defence gains 3.6 win points — also inside one standard error, in the other direction. This page used to report that ablation as a loss of 8.3 points (71.4% against 79.7%), and the build before the pass reach prints those numbers again when the same command runs on it, so what changed is the engine's ability to see the trade: the denial the AI buys is paid for with FUEL it now sometimes throws home instead of carrying. That is the third time this figure has flipped (on the build with the foul-direction bug, removing defence gained 5.2 win points at n=192, because the engine was crediting the rammer); the reading to keep is that a 96-match A/B is not enough to price defence, not that defence is free.

      Two earlier builds published 39.1% and 26.3% here, and both were the same measurement error rather than two collapses. The AI was always dealt 254/1678/1690 and its opponent always drove 4414/5940/11019, which on an identical policy is worth 59.8 points; the harness was scoring the better roster and calling it the better driver. Nothing in the policy changed when this number moved to 79.7% — the comparison got its roster confound removed. scripts/mirror.mjs is the same fix on the mirror.

      The two seats are not equivalent, so the headline averages them. Pinned to one seat over 96 MATCHes each: red 70.8% (654.6 against 629.9) and blue 78.1% (671.5 against 623.4), 7.3 win points apart — the same gap the previous build reported (69.3 / 76.6), with both seats about 3 points lower for the reason above. The page used to claim the field itself was 60 points lopsided; with the identical greedy plan on identical ROBOTs the split is −1.3 ± 7.2, so there is no seat handicap of that size to recover. The Tactics tab's selector still picks which ALLIANCE the AI plans for, and SEAT=red / SEAT=blue on ab.mjs reproduces them.

      What the search does with AUTO is the interesting part. §Table 6-3 makes the ALLIANCE that loses AUTO sit out SHIFT 1 and 3, and pinning the outcome in a same-roster mirror (n=16 per arm) says being the loser is worth about +30 points: it plays SHIFT 2 and 4 against a FUEL pool the opponent has already been eating from, and collects 35 to 50 more FUEL. Against greedy the AI scores 55.7 AUTO FUEL where greedy scores 71.5 and takes AUTO in only 25% of those MATCHes. Whether that is a deliberate throw is not established — and the same 24 MATCHes show the AI finishing 702.7 when it does win AUTO against 675.7 when it does not (n=6 against n=18), which is the confound talking: winning AUTO means your AUTO ran well, and a good AUTO is worth more than the window it costs you. The pinned mirror is the experiment that separates the two, because there the winner is forced and the AUTO quality is identical.

      It re-reads the opponent mid-SHIFT now, and that is worth saying precisely. The macro search used to run only at a period start — seven plans a MATCH, each committing 25 s — so a role assigned to a ROBOT that had already stopped moving stayed assigned, and a defence plan made against an opponent that turned out to be something else was never revisited. The search now re-runs inside a SHIFT when the class of the situation changes: a ROBOT goes, the FIELD runs out of FUEL to cycle, an assigned role becomes unexecutable, or the opponent posterior moves to a different archetype. Measured against the greedy baseline it fires essentially never and changes nothing on the build that shipped it (79.7% win, 666.3 to 622.7, the AI's own mirror 619.9 on the 4414/5940/11019 trio — the same numbers as before the feature, to a frame, and the control run reproduces them on that build today), which is the honest result: a plan made against a steady opponent is not invalidated by anything that happens next. The pass reach that landed after it moved that mirror to 606.4 and the win rate to 76.0%, so the "to a frame" claim belongs to that build, not to this one. Against an opponent that genuinely cannot be classified the same MATCHes take an extra search in half of them (8 calls instead of 7, OPP=random), because there the posterior really does move.

      The opponent is a choice. The Opponent selector beside Plan for picks who drives the other ALLIANCE: the greedy baseline (the default, and what every figure above is measured against), the shoot-always and random baselines, or the same AI on both sides. Both-sides-AI is the honest mirror of your own roster — two searches, two opponent models, roughly twice the cost per MATCH — and it is the mode scripts/mirror.mjs runs in, which is why the AI's own mirror (606.2) sits under the pinned all-SCORE one (662.0): each side spends part of its plan on denial, and in a mirror that spending cancels without either side getting the points back. Whichever mode is on, the seat you picked still decides which ALLIANCE's plan, posterior and distribution the panel reads.

      What this round's physics costs and buyssquare bodies, oriented collision, one Dumper face, turret fire on the move

      ROBOTs are now squares with a heading, so a body covers a lane according to how it is turned: the block radius is 16.7 in face-on and 22.4 in corner-on for a 110 in perimeter, plus the ball's own radius. Collisions resolve with a momentum-conserving impulse along the contact normal, split by inverse mass, at a bumper restitution of 0.12.

      Each piece is an ablation against the pre-rewrite behaviour, two seed families, n=96 per arm, off a base of 74.5% win and +37.7 of margin. Turning the whole rewrite off at once (physAllOff) costs 4.7 win points, 2.5 of score and 15.3 of margin. Removing the contact impulse (noRestitution) costs 7.8 win points and 6.0 of margin while taking only 0.9 off the score — the ROBOTS stop shoving each other off piles, so the MATCH looks the same on the scoreboard and the AI stops being able to use a body. The dark-window gate is the one that pays outright: ungating it (darkFire) costs 7.8 win points, 12.7 of score and 3.1 of margin, because a ball fired into a dead HUB is a ball the hopper was saving for the window that opens next.

      Two of these read differently than they did a build ago, and one of them reads against shipping. The Dumper face gate now helps the side that removes it: taking it off gains 2.6 win points, 11.4 of score and 12.3 of margin. It stays on because a Dumper is a hole in one face of a box, and a car that scores through its flank in a replay is a bug no statistic will catch. The turn ramp has gone flat — removing it (a ROBOT snapping its heading inside one tick) is worth 0.5 win points and 1.9 of score at n=96, which is nothing. It ships as a fidelity constraint rather than a scored one: a drivetrain with traction cannot reverse its spin in 0.1 s, and the last two builds both measured this number as a large effect in opposite directions, which is what a ±5-point binomial error does to a 96-MATCH arm.

      Throughput is the calibration anchor: the greedy mirror is 615.7 against the real Einstein average of 627, 1.8% under. The mouth's reach was set from a sweep of this same mirror rather than assumed (see "Which face the intake is on").

      Reproduce with PHYS=physAllOff,noRestitution,noFaceGate,darkFire,turnRampOff N=48 FAMILIES=90001,700001 node scripts/ab.mjs.

      The levers, measuredchassis, mechanism, driver, field size

      node scripts/lever.mjs reruns the pinned all-SCORE mirror (24 MATCHes × 2 seed sets) with one lever moved on every ROBOT at once. The label is the only difference; both sides change together.

      Chassis — swerve 629.7, mecanum 549.2, tank 446.2. A tank drive costs about 184 points, 29%, almost all of it in the turn rate: a square ROBOT has to rotate its shooting face onto the HUB, so deg/s is a cycle-time number. Mecanum used to sit within 36 points of a swerve because it strafes instead of turning to change direction; with the mouth on the back it is now 80 points behind, because the end that has to arrive first is the one its reverse leg drives at 78% of top speed.

      Mechanism — turret 685.2, dumper 591.3. A 94-point gap: a turret slews independently of the drive, so a slower chassis does not slow its aim, while a Dumper has to point the whole car.

      Driver — rookie 559.3, average 629.7, good 641.7, elite 656.9. The ladder is worth 98 points end to end, more than any mechanism or tactic setting here moves, and on this build every rung pays: good to elite is 15.2, where the previous ladder stopped at 0.9 and called the top of Table 6-1 worthless. Treat the last rung carefully — the two seed sets disagree about elite by 13.8 points on their own (650.0 and 663.8), which is nearly the size of the effect being claimed.

      Field size — 3v3 629.7, 1v1 300.9. A lone ROBOT recovers 48% of a three-ROBOT ALLIANCE. FUEL staging holds either way: §6.3.4 stages a fixed 504, so a benched ROBOT's preload goes back to the NEUTRAL ZONE.

      Why the weights have not been retrainedtwo offline runs, both refused by the holdout gate

      The job is small enough to try and too noisy to finish. One evaluation of the same weights on different seed families spreads by ±0.3 to ±0.5 fitness while a real weight change is worth about 0.1, and forty random mutants of the shipped vector average a full point worse.

      selfPlay=1, one training family, 10 generations: reported +0.91 on its training family, then measured 23 to 40 points a MATCH worse on two families it had never seen. selfPlay=0, four families, 8 generations: noise floor ±0.28, gain +0.085 — nothing — and still −4.6 points of margin on the holdout.

      So the shipped weights stay. The honest statement is that this retune needs roughly 150 MATCHes per candidate against the 32 it gets. scripts/train.mjs prints its noise floor before starting and refuses a candidate whose holdout margin does not beat twice the standard error.

      A match is a fileExport and Import on the transport bar

      Export match writes the replay that is on screen as one JSON file: the frame list, both rosters, the plan the AI chose, the scenario, the result table and the seed. Import match reads one back and replays it without running the engine or the search, so a teammate can look at the same MATCH you looked at — the scoreboard, the fouls, the per-ROBOT numbers — on their own machine, in their own browser, with no server in between.

      Frames are subsampled to one every 0.2 s (the engine records every 0.1 s tick). That is a deliberate floor, not a size saving: the renderer interpolates between anchors, and a gap wider than 0.2 s starts reading as a teleport, so a smaller file would be a worse replay. A single MATCH comes out around 3 MB — 801 anchors for the 160 s.

      The file is versioned (xplore-match-v1) and Import refuses anything else, so a future format change fails with a message instead of replaying the wrong thing. It carries no secrets — it is the same data the panel already shows.

      The replay is pinned, so a refactor cannot quietly move the ballscripts/golden.mjs

      Three fixed MATCHes are recorded once and checked on every run: a greedy mirror, a pinned mirror with hard-contact fouls on, and an AI-vs-greedy. Each gets a digest over every frame — clock, period, which HUBs are live, every ROBOT's position heading and hopper, a sample of loose FUEL, the HUMAN PLAYER, the score — at five checkpoints through the MATCH.

      It is a regression guard, not a correctness test: if a change moves the ball, it says which checkpoint moved first and what the score did, and you decide whether that was the point. node scripts/golden.mjs checks against the stored fixture and exits non-zero; --record rewrites it. The teeth were proved by mutation — moving the HUB outlet cap by 0.1 FUEL/s, which is smaller than most of the edits this page documents, drifted all three MATCHes and failed the run.

      How the engine models it

      The HUMAN PLAYERthrow while your HUB is live, dump when it is dark

      Each alliance fields one HP, staged in its OUTPOST AREA (§6.2-C). The engine runs it as an actor in its own right, off the FIELD and behind the ALLIANCE WALL: no collision body, and every ROBOT is clamped inside the wall, so no ROBOT can reach it (G421).

      Throw — the HP takes a FUEL from the CHUTE, or from a CORRAL a ROBOT filled, and throws it into their own HUB. §6.1 says so outright: HUMAN PLAYERS can deliver FUEL to ROBOTS or score them in their HUB. It aims only at its own HUB, never at an opponent ROBOT (G424); path (C) of G425. Its two settings are the two things you can measure on a person — throws per second and the percentage that go in — both per alliance at the top of the setup panel.

      Dump — the HP rotates the CHUTE DOOR about 90° and the CHUTE's ~25 FUEL slide down the 15° tunnel onto the carpet inboard of the wall (§5.9.2, path (A)). G423-A bars reaching past the tape line while the door is open, so a dump and a throwing cadence cannot run at once. G427 caps what the HP may hold off the FIELD at 2 and requires a full store to enter the FIELD at once.

      Delivery — a ROBOT that is full and cannot score drives to the OUTPOST and pushes FUEL in through the 32.0 × 7.0 in opening at the base of the wall (path (B)), and the HP picks it up on the next throw. The engine fires it only when both halves hold: our own HUB is dark and the hopper is full. Per ROBOT, Auto / Off. The CORRAL is one layer of 36 FUEL, so it refuses a ball past that and the loaded ROBOT keeps it.

      Why the rule is throw-live / dump-dark. An inactive HUB pays nothing for a throw (Tables 6-3 and 6-4), but §5.4 means the FUEL still goes through the HUB and comes out of one of four exits into the NEUTRAL ZONE — so a dark-window throw is the quickest way to move the CHUTE's 24 off the FIELD and into midfield where ROBOTs reach it.

      Not re-measured on this build. The HP's point figures this page carried (≈60 a match; rule 34% / hold 31% / always throw 43% / always dump 21% over 96 AI-vs-greedy MATCHes each) predate the square-body and chassis rewrite. They are direction only. Dumping all match was clearly the worst of the four, 22 points behind always throwing; the rule and always throwing could not be told apart. An HP that does nothing is legal and leaves all 24 FUEL off the FIELD for the whole match — set both throw rates to 0 to watch that build.

      Sweep while you shootthe roller and the shooter are separate mechanisms

      The roller keeps turning during SCORE and PASS — pickup still falls off with speed and still obeys the mouth's face band and defensive pressure — while bulldozing turns it off on purpose, since otherwise the ROBOT collects the FUEL it means to push away. In the replay the intent glyph Sc+ / Pa+ means the ROBOT picked up while shooting or passing; the + is yellow.

      What it rewards is dumping early: a Dumper that only fires when full has a full hopper, so it cannot take anything in. Over 6 MATCHes the roller runs on 52940 ticks and actually swallows a FUEL on 7591 of them (14%), and those ticks include SCORE and PASS, not just INTAKE.

      Defence really blocks shotsoriented block radius: 16.7 in face-on, 22.4 in corner-on

      A shot is a straight line from the BUMPER to the HUB mouth, so an opponent body on that line stops the ball: each tick's flight segment is swept against every opponent ROBOT. How much lane a ROBOT covers depends on how it is turned — shotBlockRadius() projects its square footprint onto the lane normal and adds the ball's radius, giving 16.7 in face-on and 22.4 in corner-on for a 110 in perimeter. SHOT_BLOCK_RADIUS_IN (22) is the corner-on worst case, for a policy that cannot know how the defender will be turned.

      A blocked ball drops as loose FUEL and can be picked up again. It scores nothing and draws no foul: G404 punishes deliberately launching at an opponent, and stepping into a shot lane is the defender's own choice. In the replay the stream line stops on the ROBOT that blocked it. The Numbers table has Shots blocked and Blocks columns.

      The model used to be blind to this event, and defenseEfficacy shipped at 0 on the argument that a value the model cannot represent should be zero rather than a guess. That argument has now been tested, and it survives — for a reason that was not the one given.

      node scripts/defcal.mjs pins BOTH sides' plans so no search is involved, sets the defender count to k, and reads the attacker's scored FUEL over 48 MATCHes per arm with one trio on both ALLIANCES. One ROBOT standing in the lane costs the attacker a factor of 0.923 (254/1678/1690) and 0.935 (4414/5940/11019); two cost 0.819 / 0.870 and three 0.695 / 0.779. The marginal defender is worth about as much as the first, because a blocked launch loses the whole round trip rather than the point — 6 launches blocked a MATCH with nobody defending, 24 with one, 45 with two, 99 with three. fastsim.js now charges defence per defender for that shape instead of one factor for the strongest one.

      The weight still ships at 0, because pricing the play made the AI worse. Setting it to the fitted value moved the dark-window mix from DEFEND 16.1% / COLLECT 63.3% to 26.6% / 54.9% — the search bought the defence it could now see — and over 96 MATCHes on four disjoint seed families that cost 9.9 points of win rate, 14.1 of score and 8.9 of margin. The diagnosis is in the two halves of the trade: the model prices what a defender takes from the opponent correctly and under-prices what it costs its own side, because a DEFEND role during your OWN inactive window looks nearly free — that ROBOT could not score anyway — when the engine is losing the whole bank of FUEL it would have carried into the window that follows. A half-priced play is worse than an unpriced one: it moves the decision without moving the ledger. Reproduce with node scripts/defcal.mjs and W=defenseEfficacy:0.1 MATCHES=8 node scripts/dark.mjs.

      Hard contact between ROBOTS: G415 / G416 / G417off by default — the fouls are priced, the card is not

      §7.4.4 is the opponent-interaction family, and all three rules need more than contact. G415 covers a ROBOT whose BUMPERS were completely outside its ALLIANCE ZONE that damages or impairs an opponent by initiating contact, G416 covers intentional or reckless damage anywhere on the FIELD, G417 covers a deliberate attach, tip or entanglement. Each is a MAJOR FOUL and a YELLOW CARD, escalating to a RED CARD when the opponent can no longer drive. Ordinary play is not a violation: G415's example A is a hit inside the PERIMETER with "no damage seen so no violation is assigned", and G416's example G is a high-speed BUMPER-to-BUMPER hit that is not reckless. So the engine assesses one only when the contact is hard enough to be a candidate and the roll says something broke, and one interaction is one foul.

      Severity is the closing speed when the BUMPERS first overlap, scaled by how much heavier the rammer is. Measured inside the engine over 10 AI-vs-greedy MATCHes (661 collisions): median 18 in/s, p90 95, p95 143, max 196. Candidates start above 130 in/s with a break chance ramping from 0 to at most 15%; a deliberate DEFEND hit above 185 tips it and above 190 knocks the drive out. A damaged ROBOT keeps 70% of its driving, shooting and intaking for 15 s; one that cannot drive is at zero for 20 s, the manual's own "approximately ~20+ seconds".

      The break probability is the one guess. Nothing in the manual says how often a hard hit damages a ROBOT, so it is calibrated to land near what the real finals show: 0, 1 and 2 fouls a MATCH across the three Einstein finals.

      Two gaps. G417's entanglement half is not modelled — hooking two ROBOTS together needs a mechanism this 2-D model does not have. And the engine records the card without pricing it: a second YELLOW in a phase is a RED, which is a DISQUALIFICATION, and that is the deterrent that stops teams ramming. Without it a 15-point foul buys 20 s of an impaired opponent, which measures as a good trade. That is why the family ships off.

      The MCTS fast model does not see contact fouls at all, so the AI neither seeks nor avoids them. On the score the switch is close to free: over both sample trios and 48 MATCHes per arm, the AI's own mirror moves from 616.4 to 619.5 with the fouls on, 3 points, which is inside the ±2% a mirror carries. The greedy mirror on the same runs moves 1.7 (617.4 against the 615.7 anchor) — the hand-written cycling plan barely ever makes a deliberate hit, which is the case the rules actually charge.

      Who is charged, and a bug that paid the wrong side. Every foul the engine logs carries the ROBOT that committed it, the ALLIANCE it was assessed against and the clause, and the Numbers tab now lists them in match order with a jump to the second it happened; the batch view averages the same thing per ROBOT. Building that list is what found the bug: G418 (a PIN over 3 s), G419 (closing the traversal corridors) and G415/G416/G417 were all being charged to the victim's alliance, so the side that pinned, blocked the band or rammed an opponent kept the points. G408, G420 and G427 were right. The verifier's own check only compared the label on the event with the offender's colour, so it passed while the arithmetic handed the points the wrong way. Both are fixed: the engine charges the offender, and the guard now asserts that the foul is assessed against the rammer's own ALLIANCE. It is worth saying what that changed about the conclusion — with the bug, the engine was paying the AI for fouls, so the ablation said removing DEFEND gained 5.2 win points at n=192. Charge the fouls where the rules charge them and the same ablation costs 8.3 win points on the current build: defence does pay in the engine, and the planner is kept from spending on it — see "Defence really blocks shots" for what pricing it measured as a loss.

      Passing and bulldozingper ROBOT, and the search does not price it

      These are tactical choices rather than specs, so they sit on each ROBOT's card. Passing: Off, or one of two postures that share a target list — a teammate on our own half with room to take the load, or, when nobody is home, empty ground inside our own ALLIANCE ZONE — and both are capped by this ROBOT's pass range. What separates them is how much the throw has to be worth: Careful wants a hopper 85% full and a saving of at least 150 in over driving it home, Aggressive settles for a third of a hopper and 60 in. Either way the landing spot avoids the HUB mouth and the outlet area, so collecting it back does not draw G408. Bulldozing: Off / Passive (push only on contact) / Active (drive around the far side of loose FUEL and shove it back toward your half).

      How far a pass carries is a spec, not a choice, so it is a number on the card (Pass range in, next to Accuracy) rather than a rule. The shooter throws with one fixed punch: a partner beyond that reach is not reached, and the FUEL comes down short on the same line, loose, on whoever's carpet it landed on. The geometry is why this is the interesting one — the FIELD is 651 in long, the front edges of the two ALLIANCE ZONES are 334 in apart and their middle lines 493 in (§5.2/§5.3), so handing a load from inside the opponent's zone back to your own is a 400-500 in throw. A long-range shooter gets that play; a 260 in one does not, and has to drive it home or find a teammate nearer midfield. No rule is involved: §5.3 lets a ROBOT drive into the opponent's zone once the CENTER LINE is down, and G407 only governs a launch into the HUB.

      A pass scores nothing (landing bonus is always 0) and a target inside 40 in of your own HUB is rejected. Whether you may pass at all comes down to the scope of G407: the TU21 text (p.65) is titled "Only score while in your ALLIANCE ZONE" and its body reads only A ROBOT may not launch a SCORING ELEMENT into their HUB unless their BUMPERS are partially or fully within their ALLIANCE ZONE. That covers the shot into the HUB, not a ball thrown elsewhere, so passing is not under G407. G404 and G405 do apply, and the engine satisfies both by construction: the target is a teammate in your own half or a spot in your ALLIANCE ZONE, never an opponent, clamped inside the boundary.

      Measured, with a control. MATCHES=48 node scripts/range.mjs throws the same trio (254/1678/1690) at four reaches against a fifth arm whose reach nobody can exceed — i.e. the pass unconstrained, which is how this engine behaved before the field existed. At 520 in: 14,445 passes, none of them coming down more than 508 in from the ROBOT that threw it (the cap plus at most one tick of flight), and 93 of them cross from inside the opponent's ALLIANCE ZONE into our own. At 260 in: 9,392 passes, furthest landing 267 in, 0 cross-zone. At 140 in: 3,569 passes, furthest 147 in, none of them reach their own zone at all. The uncapped arm lands 101 cross-zone throws and a furthest 539 in, so the play is real and the reach is what decides who gets it. Under Careful, measured separately at 24 MATCHes, the direct throw home now fires too: 276 passes, 198 of them coming down inside our own ALLIANCE ZONE, furthest 453 in, at the same score as passing switched off (641.3 ± 9.1 against 640.7 ± 10.1) — and the reach still decides, since a 260 in shooter keeps only 59 of those throws and not one reaches home, while a 140 in one never passes at all. What the 85%-of-a-hopper bar costs is the deepest version of the play: none of the Careful throws were launched from inside the opponent's zone, because a ROBOT that full that far in has usually already thrown. What does move is the Aggressive price: 533.9 ± 7.6 at a 520 in reach against 642.6 ± 6.9 with passing off, so that posture is a 109-point loss on this build.

      Bulldozing pushes loose FUEL within 22 in along the direction of travel, with force proportional to speed (0.8), on the grounds that G408 explicitly excludes "bulldozing" and "deflecting" from CONTROL. FUEL is stopped by structures the same way a ROBOT is, so nothing gets pushed inside the TOWER body — otherwise a ball nobody can reach pins a ROBOT for a whole MATCH, a trap the engine fell into during testing. A ROBOT bulldozing on purpose does not run its roller, or it would collect instead of push.

      The micro policy passes; the search still does not price it. The fast model cannot value a throw, so no plan is built around one and the macro layer treats passing as a per-ROBOT habit rather than an objective. That used to mean the AI almost never passed — now that Careful may aim at home too, the shipped default throws 276 times in 24 MATCHes instead of 82, and "How strong the AI is" carries what that cost (79.7% to 76.0% on the same seeds). The older figures this note carried — loosening Careful dropped the win rate from 25% to 6%, bulldozing is worth about 99 on the mirror — predate the square-body rewrite and have not been re-measured. The price of Aggressive has: 109 points a MATCH at a 520 in reach, and it falls as the reach falls (566.5 ± 8.0 at 260 in, 606.1 ± 6.5 at 140 in, against 642.6 ± 6.9 with passing off).

      Square bodies, chassis and turn ratenew fields on every ROBOT card

      A ROBOT is a square now — drawn with rounded corners, its footprint half-width perimeterIn / 8 — and its heading is first-class state, integrated each tick under a turn-rate limit rather than snapped to the direction of travel.

      Chassis is per ROBOT and sets three multipliers on top of its drive speed. Swerve translates in any direction and spins in place at full rate: lateral 1.0, reverse 1.0, turn-at-speed 1.0, reference 540 deg/s. Tank drive drives only along its heading or reversed: lateral 0, reverse 0.6, turn-at-speed 0.55, reference 300 deg/s. Mecanum strafes without turning but loses grip doing it: lateral 0.62, reverse 0.78, turn-at-speed 0.75, reference 200 deg/s — all four wheels scrub sideways, so rotating in place is its weakest move.

      Turn rate deg/s is its own editable field. Changing the chassis resets it to that chassis's reference — unless the number in the box is one you typed yourself, in which case it is a measurement and is left alone.

      The peak rate is a ceiling, not a step. Each chassis also carries a ramp time — swerve 0.25 s, tank 0.35 s, mecanum 0.30 s — and the engine integrates the spin rate against it, so a turn is a trapezoid: a drivetrain has to build the spin up against the carpet and bleed it off again, and it cannot reverse direction inside one tick. Those three ramp times are estimates from what each drivetrain has to do physically, not measurements of a specific robot; the peak rates are the ones a card can override. Ablating the ramp (PHYS=turnRampOff, n=96 on this build) — letting a ROBOT snap its heading inside a tick — is worth 0.5 win points and 1.9 of score, which is nothing. It ships because a drivetrain with traction cannot reverse its spin in 0.1 s and an instant rotation is exactly what a replay shows as fake, not because a measurement asked for it: the last two builds each priced this at a several-point effect in opposite directions.

      Both matter because a square ROBOT with one shooting face has to rotate onto the HUB. On the pinned mirror a tank drive costs 184 points against a swerve (446.2 to 629.7). Mecanum sits 80 points under a swerve at 549.2: it strafes rather than turning to change direction, which used to make it nearly as good as a swerve, until the intake mouth moved to the back face and its 78% reverse leg became the end that has to arrive first.

      Which face the intake is ona full-width slot, and Back is the default

      The mouth is a slot across the whole face, which is what a roller intake actually is: bumper to bumper, not a narrow opening. The engine's test is a band, not a cone — FUEL is inside the mouth if it lies within the car's own width across the face and within MOUTH_REACH_IN of the face line. The card offers Front, Back, Left side and Right side, the replay draws the teal bar on whichever face you picked, and the engine rotates the same band by the same bearing, so the picture and the physics cannot drift apart.

      Back is the default on every ROBOT now. A Front mouth and a front-mounted Dumper shooter want the same 24 in of bumper — one edge cannot carry a roller and an opening at once — so the default moved to the face that is free. A turret car has no fixed shooting face and may still put the roller in front; that is a choice on the card now, not the assumption underneath every number on this page.

      The reach is the one guessed number, and the mirror says the guess is conservative. Width comes from the chassis and is not a guess; how far behind the face line the rollers still grab is. Sweeping it over the same-trio greedy mirror, 192 MATCHes per point: 5 in 594.1, 6 in 615.7, 7 in 612.9, 8 in 617.6, 10 in 625.8, 12 in 632.1 — against the real Einstein Finals average of 627. Six inches ships because that is roughly what a ball caught against a bumper is, not because the mirror prefers it; a 12-in mouth would score 16 points more and overshoot the real average. The value is stated as an estimate and pinned by scripts/golden.mjs so that a refactor cannot silently change it.

      What follows from a face is that a ROBOT sweeps a pile by leading with the mouth. On a COLLECT trip the car turns the intake face toward where it is going and trails the rest behind it, so a rear intake backs into every pile at the chassis's reverse fraction of top speed, and a side intake has to strafe. STOCKPILE is exempt — a positioning move, and a ROBOT hurrying home before its HUB opens should not have to reverse the whole way. PUSH is exempt because a pusher wants a BUMPER on the pile, never the mouth. FEED is exempt too, and that one was caught by a test: its destination is a wall opening the car has to park beside, and leading with a rear mouth backed the ROBOT into the OUTPOST and lost the CORRAL mouth — delivery fell to zero.

      On a swerve the face barely registers. Pinned mirror, 24 MATCHes × 2 seed sets: 626.6 / 629.7 / 627.9 / 623.9 front to right — a 5.8-point spread on 630, inside the noise floor. A swerve strafes and spins in place at full rate, so it can lead with any face at almost any speed.

      A tank drive is where it bites, but only sideways: 446.6 front, 446.2 back, 397.8 left, 407.3 right. Sides cost 39 to 49 points because a tank has no strafe to lead with — it keeps driving nose-first and only swallows what the flank brushes past. Front and back are now the same number, 0.4 apart. That is a correction, not a rounding: on the previous layout the rear mouth measured worth +20 on a tank, and what it was actually measuring was the NEUTRAL ZONE, whose FUEL lattice sat closer to one end than the other. Symmetric midfield, no face preference.

      So why is Back the default? Not because the mirror pays for it — on this build it does not. It is the bumper argument above: a Front slot and a front-mounted Dumper shooter want the same edge, and the default should sit on the face that is free on both mechanisms. The chassis that would notice a face most is mecanum, which leads with a face its reverse leg only reaches at 78% of top speed; its own face sweep has not been re-run since the layout changed.

      Where each ROBOT starts§5.3 STARTING LINE, G303-D

      §5.3 defines the ROBOT STARTING LINE as "an ALLIANCE colored line that spans the width of the FIELD at the edge of an ALLIANCE ZONE in front of two BUMPS and an ALLIANCE HUB", and G303-D makes every ROBOT start with its BUMPERS overlapping that line. Each card carries a Starting position: Auto gives the three DRIVER STATIONS the three structures the line runs in front of — 1 at a BUMP, 2 at the HUB, 3 at the other BUMP — and you can override any ROBOT to In front of a BUMP, Under a TRENCH or In front of the HUB. Two ROBOTS told to take the same lane line up 34 in apart instead of starting on top of each other.

      One honest deviation: this engine's BUMPS, TRENCHES and HUB straddle the ALLIANCE ZONE line rather than sitting beyond it, so a ROBOT centred on the line would stand inside the structure in its lane. Each start is therefore set 38 in inside the line, as far forward as its lane allows, and the verifier checks both the lane and that the start is inside the ALLIANCE ZONE and clear of the band.

      Who is driving, and who is on the fieldthe driver is the biggest single lever on this page

      Driver is the person in the box (Table 6-1: a DRIVE TEAM's DRIVERS are STUDENTs, up to 3 per team, shared between driving and the HUMAN PLAYER role). It moves the three things a driver actually controls: aim, how long the mechanism takes to come on target, and how much they get out of the ROBOT on defence. Rookie takes 0.10 off a reference driver's shot accuracy and needs half again as long to line up; Elite adds 0.05 and lines up in 70% of the time. Aim is a flat offset rather than a multiplier because every reference ROBOT already sits at 0.92–0.93, so multiplying would pin them all at 1.000 and erase the difference between two good teams. Average is the reference the rest of this page is measured on. Driving itself is not scaled: the engine's path following is closed-loop, so the "driver" in that loop is the controller, and the AI plans against the same effective numbers.

      Measured on the pinned mirror, 24 MATCHes × 2 seed sets, the ladder is 559.3 rookie / 629.7 average / 641.7 good / 656.9 elite — 98 points end to end, and unlike the previous build every rung pays (good to elite is 15.2). node scripts/lever.mjs reproduces it.

      Off the field benches a ROBOT: no start position, no preload, never in the replay, and the DRIVER STATIONS close up around it. Field one a side and the MATCH is a 1v1 — a simulation setting, not a legal configuration, since in a real MATCH every team fields its ROBOT. Measured the same way, a 1v1 scores 300.9 against the 3v3's 629.7, so a lone ROBOT recovers 48%. FUEL staging holds either way: §6.3.4 stages a fixed 504, so a benched ROBOT's 8-FUEL preload moves back into the NEUTRAL ZONE instead of the FIELD shrinking.

      The inactive window: no idle rule, no wasted shots50 s an alliance cannot score, spent as an ordered ladder

      Under Table 6-3 the alliance that loses AUTO is inactive in SHIFT 1 and 3, and the winner in SHIFT 2 and 4, so every alliance has 50 seconds where it cannot score. The AI turns that into an ordered ladder: bulldoze (if set to Active, use the free time to push loose FUEL back) → sweep (keep loading if there is room) → come home (inside darkPrepSec = 4 s of the HUB going live, a ROBOT that is carrying stops whatever it was doing and holds its own shooting lane) → deliver to the HUMAN PLAYER → defend → pass → block → hold position. Even the last rung is not IDLE, and a full ROBOT never pretends to sweep — a full intake cannot take anything in. After a MATCH, What the match tells you reports how that stretch was spent.

      Nothing scores into a dead HUB. A FUEL that goes in during an INACTIVE window still passes through the HUB and out into the NEUTRAL ZONE (§5.4), so it is relocated, not deleted — but it scores 0, and it is 0 the alliance paid to carry. The engine used to volunteer one such shot on its own: a turret sweeping past its reference distance fired opportunistically with no check on whether its HUB was live, which emptied the hopper the policy was banking for the next window. That shot is now gated. What is not gated is a policy that deliberately dumps into a dead HUB — that is a choice, it is legal, and the greedy baseline keeps making it.

      The come-home rung is worth 10.9 points of win rate. Re-measured on this build over four disjoint seed families, n=96 per arm (ARMS=base,noDarkPrep): switching it off drops the AI from 79.7% to 68.8%, from 666.3 to 648.2 a MATCH, and the margin from 43.5 to 32.8 — every one of the four families goes down, and a two-family sample earlier in the same build read the same direction at −9.4 / −13.3 / −17.3. Doubling the lead to 8 s gains 1.6 win points and gives back 3.2 of score, so 4 s still ships.

      Two time-aware ideas lost and stayed out. The first is the one you would expect to win: gating the dark-window sweep on "can this pickup AND the leg home fit in the time left". On the proxy it worked exactly as promised — the FUEL carried by a ROBOT standing off its own shooting lane at the reopen fell from 37.8% to 14.3% (node scripts/cap.mjs) — and it cost 5.7 points of win rate over 96 MATCHes with all four families down or flat. Being home in position is worth less than the load you did not pick up to get there, because the load is what the window is for. The second was lateTripPenalty, which priced a pile by whether its round trip overshot the live window: it moved the proxy from 104 in to 92 in and cost 5.7 win points on the build it was added to, and its code is gone rather than pinned at zero. Both failures point the same way, and it is not a defect in the AI's clock — the dump rules already read secsUntilHubOff, and on the tail of a live window the AI leaves only 1.5% of frames in an unfinished trip where the hand-written baseline leaves 2.0%, and is caught holding 14.0 FUEL at a window close where the baseline holds 21.4. The clock is fine. What the model cannot yet price is the value of arriving late with more.

      Loading sits ahead of defending in that ladder: a ROBOT with room in its hopper keeps sweeping instead of switching to defence at 81% full. What has not changed is that defence is not where the raw points are — its payoff is denial, and the engine has to charge the fouls to the rammer for that to show up (see "Hard contact between ROBOTS").

      Reproduce with MATCHES=8 node scripts/dark.mjs, which tallies the intent mix, the shots fired at a dead HUB and the passes thrown during every INACTIVE frame, for the AI and the baseline separately; node scripts/gap.mjs prints the same two drivers' collected / scored / wasted / distance side by side; and ARMS=base PREPS=0,4,8 N=48 FAMILIES=90001,700001 node scripts/ab.mjs sweeps the come-home rung.

      Teamwork, and one time-aware idea that losttwo ROBOTS on one pile; a rejected weight

      The defect was measurable before it was fixed. On the build it was found on, over 6 MATCHes against greedy, scripts/team.mjs counted the AI's ROBOTS within a bumper of each other on 22.1% of frames and driving at the same pile on 6.7%, and a ROBOT carrying FUEL was on average 104 in from its own HUB when the window opened. Greedy, with no notion of a partner at all, was worse on every one of those: 33.8% / 14.6% / 146 in. So the AI was already the better teammate and still bunching a fifth of the time.

      Two ROBOTS on one pile do not empty it any faster and spend the trip shoving each other. mateSeparation = 0.6 prices a candidate pile down by how close a partner is — measured both to the partner's body and to the target the partner is already driving at, because a partner 200 in away is still going to arrive first. The penalty falls to nothing at MATE_SEP_IN = 72, about two car widths, so it spreads the ALLIANCE out without forbidding two ROBOTS from ever being near each other. Zero turns it off and leaves only the per-tick grid claim, which is the control it was measured against.

      Re-measured on this build the ablation is flat, and it ships anyway. Turning it off moves win rate +0.5, score +0.5 and margin +1.7 at n=96 (two families) — every one of those inside what a two-family sample can resolve. On the build it was introduced on the same ablation read −5.0 of score and −5.3 of margin, so the term has since stopped being where the points are. What keeps it in is the proxy, not the A/B: over 6 MATCHes against greedy the AI's ROBOTS now bunch on 14.6% of frames and double-tap a pile on 4.1%, against greedy's 30.3% and 9.9%, and a carrying ROBOT sits 87 in from its own HUB when a window opens where greedy sits 179 in. The bunching series on the AI side is 22.1% → 20.1% → 14.6% across the three builds, which is the effect the weight was bought for.

      One time-aware knob was built, measured and deleted. lateTripPenalty priced down a pile whose round trip would overshoot the live window, which is the obvious thing to try. It worked on the proxy: caught-far fell from 104 to 92 in. It cost 5.7 win points at n=96, on the build it was added to. The reason is that banking a load into the dark window is not a loss — darkPrepSec brings the ROBOT home and it scores on the tick the HUB reopens, so the penalty was steering ROBOTS off trips that were already fine. The weight, its bound and its code are gone rather than shipped pinned at zero. The proxy metric moved and the win rate went the other way; that is the whole argument for measuring every knob against a control.

      Reproduce with MATCHES=6 node scripts/team.mjs and ARMS=base SWEEP="mateSeparation:0" N=48 FAMILIES=90001,700001 node scripts/ab.mjs.

      Crossing the field (BUMP / TRENCH)§5.7 height rule

      The default follows §5.7: a ROBOT at or below 22.25 in can take the TRENCH. The crossing control on each card can be set to TRENCH + BUMP / BUMP only / TRENCH only / Neither, to record what you scouted — say, a ROBOT short enough but still unable to climb the BUMP. If the setting conflicts with the height rule the card says so, so you can tell a deliberate entry from a mistake. A ROBOT that can cross neither is held out of the corridor band on its own side. That is the engine's honest result, not a bug.

      Turret or Dumper94 points apart, and they now fail differently

      A turret slews onto the HUB independently of the chassis, so it fires from anywhere in its ALLIANCE ZONE, while still rolling, with almost no time to come on target (0.1 s). A full-width Dumper has to reach the HUB face, stop, and wait out a much longer spin-up (1.5 s) before the whole hopper leaves at once.

      These followed from making the body a square. The Dumper has one shooting face: it scores only through the pale edge drawn on the replay, within DUMPER_FACE_TOL_DEG = 20° of the bearing to the HUB. Turning is part of its cycle time, so its turn rate is a scoring stat. The gate is priced rather than sold: taking it off (PHYS=noFaceGate, n=96 on this build) gains the AI 2.6 win points, 11.4 of score and 12.3 of margin, so on this engine the constraint costs its own side points and stays only because a box that scores through its flank is not a Dumper. On the build before it the same arm gained 4.5 of score but cost 11.8 of margin, because the baseline gained 16.3 — the gate has never measured as free, and it has measured both ways. The turret fires on the move: within TURRET_OPPORTUNITY_MARGIN_IN = 70 in past its reference distance it takes a shot it did not stop for, because a miss costs little — the ball comes down as loose FUEL and is re-collectable. An earlier build reported a clean ladder over that margin, and it did not survive re-measurement: sweeping 0 / 10 / 20 / 40 / 70 gave 41.7 / 33.9 / 35.9 / 39.6 / 40.6% on the build before this one, spread without a trend, with the top of it sitting on the value that switches the behaviour off. So 70 ships as a physical reach — how far past its lane a turret can still hit — and not as a sweep winner, and that figure is labelled as the older measurement rather than restated as current.

      On the pinned mirror the gap is turret 685.2 against dumper 591.3, 94 points — wider than the 49 an earlier build reported, because a turret slews independently of the drive, so the turn limit does not slow its aim, while a Dumper has to point the whole car. The turret's own aim time barely matters; its score did not move between 0 and 0.15 s, also from the earlier build.

      The net behind the HUB, and FUEL that never stacks§5.4 and §5.10.1

      §5.4: "A net structure located in the back of the HUB prevents FUEL launched from most prohibited areas from entering the opening." The prohibited areas are outside your own ALLIANCE ZONE and the HUB sits exactly on the zone line, so a ROBOT firing at its own HUB from behind it is shooting into the net. The engine enforces this where it is cheapest and most honest: the launch never leaves the hopper, so the ROBOT keeps its load and drives back in, and G407 stays a rule rather than a foul the AI farms. The same net stops a shot that simply misses — the opening sits on a solid prism, so a ball that does not go in cannot come out the far side. It strikes the structure and comes down at the base of the face it hit, in the shooter's own ALLIANCE ZONE. The old code dropped a missed shot 20–95 in past the HUB centre; over two greedy mirrors 43 of 60 misses used to come to rest beyond that edge, the worst 72 in past it, and with the net catching them none do. The verifier pins both halves. The net is not drawn; it is a physical gate, not furniture.

      Two FUEL are balls on the carpet and cannot share a point. §5.10.1 makes each one a 5.91 in foam ball, and every landing is an independent random draw, so balls used to land stacked — which read as a rendering bug and let one sweep take two off the same inch. Every landing now goes through one placement helper: expel the ball from any structure it came down inside, then nudge it off any loose ball it would overlap to one diameter apart. The opening layout was the worst case, because the 206 × 72 in NEUTRAL ZONE box holds 360 FUEL — past the density where a uniform scatter jams — so its fallback spot now goes down on a jittered hexagonal lattice one diameter across, which reads as a corral rather than a grid. It is a placement fix, not a continuous separation pass, so it does not stir the piles the MATCH itself builds.
